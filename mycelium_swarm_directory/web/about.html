Our vision

AI that belongs to the people who use it

Simple

The short version

A handful of companies own AI, and almost everyone else rents it from them. They own the chips, the models and the rules. They set the price, decide what AI can do, see everything you send, and can cut you off. If you live somewhere they don't care about, you were never invited to the conversation.

That isn't a law of nature. Someone chose it, and it can be unchosen. We're building AI owned by the people who use it.

How it works

The best AI models are too big for any one ordinary computer, which is why almost everyone rents. Mycelium cuts a model into pieces and spreads them across laptops, desktops and home servers people already own. Each device handles its slice and passes the result on, so together they run what none could run alone. We call a group of devices working together a swarm.

Sharing is capped by design, so your own apps always come first. It's your machine, not ours.

Today: this already works. Our open-source preview runs models across several devices, and you can download it for Apple-silicon Macs. We've also run it on a Linux laptop and, in lab tests, a phone.

Who's in charge

Nobody, in the way you're used to. The people who share their devices decide. Groups set their own rules, choose how they make decisions, link up with others, or walk away and take their devices with them. We build tools, not thrones, and we're designing so that no permanent rulers can form.

Today: the person who starts a swarm coordinates it. We're working towards swarms where any member can keep things running.

Access is not charity

Whether you can use AI shouldn't depend on whether you can pay. As swarms connect, part of what's paid into the network will fund a shared AI Commons that gives free basic access to people who can't afford it, starting with the Global South and communities shut out of decisions about AI. People whose devices do the work will be paid fairly, and that fairness is built into how money moves, not left to the goodwill of the richest.

We're not here to make money off the people who use it. We take no venture capital, so there are no investors to repay, and we plan to become a non-profit cooperative. The code is open source under a licence that stops anyone taking it private.

Today: swarms are private groups with no payments. The Commons is designed, not built.

Built for the people usually left out

People get locked out of technology in layers: income, language, gender, disability, location and connection. We design for the margins first. You won't need expensive hardware to join, small models can run offline where connections are poor, and we want models built in local languages with the communities who use them.

And nobody has to join anything bigger. A community can run its own swarm as a closed circle of people who trust each other, with no payments, no identity checks and no link to the wider network. Joining is always a choice, never a condition.

What we haven't solved

Systems that promise no rulers tend to grow them anyway, and we don't claim to have escaped that.

Running AI on other people's devices raises hard questions. Did the right model answer, and who saw your prompt? Nobody has fully solved this for a model split across many machines, and with today's AI services you can't check either: you take their word for it. We're building the checks in stages, starting with a detector that told real answers from imitations 90–95% of the time in early tests on one model.

Today, the devices in your swarm can see what they process, so only share a swarm with people you trust.

Two more problems are open. Proving that one person is one person, without demanding papers or exposing who they are, has no clean answer yet. And an open network can be misused: a released model can't be recalled, so stopping harmful uses without building a gatekeeper is something we're still working out. We'll say plainly what's solved and what isn't.

Part of something bigger

Mycelium is the computing layer of CCAI (Collective Commons AI), a working title for the wider project: shared computing, a commons for access, and the tools communities use to govern it together.

Technical

The problem, precisely

Inference is concentrated in a few providers. That creates dependence (access and model behaviour set by others), cost (prices set for profit, not cost) and exposure (prompts and documents handed to a remote operator, with no way to check which model answered or who saw it). Concentration is also a systemic risk: one decision, takeover or outage affects everyone.

How Mycelium works

Mycelium runs one model across several devices by splitting it into consecutive slices of layers, a pipeline. Every step below is built: it is how today's preview works, tested so far on the project's own devices.

  1. A swarm forms. One device creates the swarm and its signing keys. Others join with a short-lived, single-use invitation, and each new device is approved by its key fingerprint. Every device keeps its own identity; there are no accounts.
  2. Devices offer what they can, on their own terms. Each member sets consent limits: how long a run may last, and how much memory, storage, power and network it may use. Runs renew automatically before the shortest limit, so sharing stays within what each owner agreed to.
  3. The model is cut into stages. A planner splits the model's layers into consecutive ranges and places each range on a device according to the memory it offers, refusing plainly if no split fits. Each device downloads the weights it needs, checked against pinned hashes.
  4. A request flows from device to device. Your prompt goes to the swarm's entry device, which turns it into numbers and runs the first stage. Each stage hands its output, the activations, to the next device: arrays of numbers, not your files. They travel over encrypted peer-to-peer QUIC connections, directly on a local network or through a relay across the internet.
  5. The answer streams back. The last stage chooses the next word, and the answer streams back to you word by word. Each new word takes one trip through the whole pipeline, so the links between devices matter as much as the devices themselves.
  6. Devices can stop or leave. When a device reaches a limit, pauses or leaves, the swarm re-plans the stages across the devices that remain. A standby device can take over an answer mid-way with identical output, and every request ends plainly as completed, cancelled or failed with a reason, never a fake success.

Today: the device that starts a swarm admits its members and is the entry point for every request, so it sees every prompt. The other devices see the activations they process.

What runs today

  • An early preview (v0.1.4) for Apple-silicon Macs, with Qwen models. In development runs it has also run on a Linux laptop, and a phone computed a pipeline stage in a lab test.
  • Signed, opt-in listing. A swarm can choose to appear in the public directory on mycelium.now with a signed listing that it withdraws when it stops.
  • v0.2, in progress: a full Rust rewrite with the same behaviour and Qwen models: a Tauri desktop app, Iroh for the encrypted QUIC links, and the layer-split pipeline running on MLX over Metal.

Honest limits today

  • Not private from your swarm. Devices doing the work can see what they process. Use swarms with people you trust.
  • Not verified. You can't yet prove which model produced an answer.
  • One coordinator per swarm. The device that starts a swarm admits members and routes requests.
  • Not automatically cheaper or greener. Distribution reduces dependence on central providers; it doesn't by itself make inference more efficient.

What we're building towards

This is direction, not a promise: none of it is dated, and some of it is research.

  • Any open model. A universal model adapter that reads a model's published configuration, builds it from shared parts and checks its output against a reference, instead of hand-written code for each model. Anything it can't run yet is refused with a reason.
  • Smarter routing. Devices announce which layers they hold, new members are placed as they join, and each request takes its own route through the swarm. A model that fits on one device runs there alone, and when a device leaves, its work moves to the others without starting over.
  • More platforms, bigger models. Windows and Linux machines with their graphics cards, phones as pipeline stages, and later browsers. Bigger models by streaming parts of mixture-of-experts models from disk and sizing work to the swarm's pooled memory.
  • More private by design. The first and last layers, where words become numbers and numbers become words, stay on the device that asked, so the text of your prompt and answer doesn't leave it. A published threat model will say plainly what the activations can still reveal.
  • Verification, in stages. Statistical model-attribution checks (early detectors: 90–95% correct on unseen topics), sampled spot checks, and trusted hardware where available. The long-term goal is proof-based verification. Proving even a small model currently takes about 30 hours on consumer hardware, so this is research.
  • No special owner. Swarm-wide jobs such as coordinating, publishing the listing or relaying become roles: leased, recallable and rotated, and any eligible member can hold one. Each member signs its own record, and each request is coordinated by the device that sends it.
  • Open swarms and federation. Each swarm chooses how people join: invitation with approval, anyone with a link, or open, with the app stating plainly what each choice exposes. Swarms can be discovered and can link up across trust boundaries. Opening up to strangers depends on the privacy and verification work.
  • Fair shares. Credits earned from the work a device actually does, and lending spare capacity between swarms.
  • Standard interfaces. OpenAI-compatible and Ollama-compatible APIs and bridges for coding agents, so the tools people already use can run on a swarm.
  • Community fine-tuning. Swarms adapting models together on the devices that hold them. This is research.

Principles

  • Decide locally
  • Voluntary association, with the right to leave or fork and take your contribution with you
  • Federation over hierarchy
  • No permanent rulers: roles rotate and can be recalled
  • Fairness built into the economics
  • Open source (AGPL-3.0) and open-weight models
  • No venture capital: capital can't buy control
  • Honest about what's built, designed and still research
